### **Case 1: Yugoslavia (1989-1995) — Debt-Driven Cascading Fragmentation**

**Economic Context:**

Yugoslavia incurred $21 billion in external debt in the 1970s-80s. IMF conditions forced austerity. Real earnings fell 25% between 1979-1985. Real GDP growth had collapsed from 5.8% (1940s-60s) to 2.6% (1970s) to near-zero by the 1980s.

By 1987-88, strikes erupted nationwide as workers demanded wage increases to compensate for inflation. The "Agrokomerc affair" (a $1 billion fraudulent promissory note scheme) exposed massive corruption and destroyed elite legitimacy.

**Political Fission Mechanism:**

Economic austerity intensified regional tensions. Wealthy republics (Slovenia, Croatia) felt they subsidized poorer ones (Serbia, Bosnia). By 1990, these republics began declaring sovereignty. Slovenia and Croatia held independence referendums and declared independence on June 25, 1991.

**Military Breakdown:**

As the federal government weakened, the Yugoslav National Army (JNA) fragmented. Nationalism became the tool for local political elites to consolidate power in their regions. Military units divided along ethnic lines; army leadership could no longer enforce central command.

**Violence Cascade:**

Serb minorities in Croatia and Bosnia rebelled with JNA support, declaring autonomous zones. This triggered ethnic cleansing, militia formation, and **complete loss of state monopoly on violence.**

**Relevance to Iran:**
