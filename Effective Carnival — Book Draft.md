## Preface — Trust Your Gut

People of Earth, trust your gut. It has been telling you something for years and you have been talking over it.

The Earth is sick. You feel it in the air you pay to filter and the water you pay to bottle. You feel it in the way a day can pass in which every sentence you spoke was shaped by a screen, and in the way a screen now seems to know what you will say before you do. We were promised that technology would be a tool. Somewhere along the way the arrangement quietly reversed. The tree of progress stopped serving humanity, and humanity began to serve the tree.

People reach for old words when the new ones fail them. Some say the devil has always been watching. Some say we handed a demon our attention and it never gave it back. You do not have to share anyone’s theology to feel the force of the image: a thing that watches, never sleeps, and profits from your distraction. Call it what you like. The feeling is the data.

I want to be careful here, because rage is easy and accuracy is hard. This book is angry, but it is not careless. Where I give you a number, I will tell you where it came from. Where I give you a suspicion, I will call it a suspicion. And where I give you a scenario, I will mark it as one, so that you can argue with it rather than merely believe it.

Three things are true at once, and the whole book lives in the tension between them.

**First**, the planet cannot carry the way we live. This is not an argument against children, or love, or joy. Please, mingle. Make love. Make families, make mess, make noise. Life is not the problem. The problem is an economy that treats the living world as a quarry and every human being as a customer.

**Second**, we are not in a film. There is no cosmic simulation to blame, no hidden programmer to petition. What there is, is a consciousness, ours, a small and astonishing light, and it is worth preserving. But preserving it means supporting the living planet that grew it. Gaia is not a metaphor for sentimental people. She is the life-support system.

**Third**, distant institutions that nobody elected and nobody can question have learned to extract from ordinary people with astonishing efficiency. Whether you place the blame on governments, corporations, or the fusion of the two, the pattern is the same: the people who bear the costs are never the ones who made the decisions.

And then there is Iran, which is the book’s wound and its case study. A country of ninety million people, a civilization older than most of the maps we use to describe it, living through what may be the most concentrated collapse of our time: war, a currency that lost its meaning, hunger, a government that tightens its grip as the floor gives way, and an exodus of its most educated young people, with the strange, quiet counter-current of those who come back.

I do not write about Iran as an outsider tourist of misery. I write because what is happening there is a preview. Whatever you think of the Islamic Republic, and I think it has done terrible things to its own people, the mechanics of the collapse are not exotic. They are portable. They can happen to a petrostate, a banking system, or a language. Learn them there and you will recognize them elsewhere.

# Part I — The Locust Age

## Chapter 1 — We Built the Machine That Serves Itself

There is a number that should keep us awake: twenty billion.

That is roughly the count of connected devices now woven through human life, more than two for every living person, and the count keeps climbing. Not twenty billion people. Twenty billion machines that listen, measure, route, price, and predict. When the first humans painted animals on cave walls, the animals outnumbered the painters by an absurd margin. Today the machines outnumber the humans. We are, in a very literal sense, the minority species in our own habitat.

None of this is evil by itself. A thermostat is not a demon. But scale changes the moral weather. A tool that you pick up and put down is a tool. A system that you cannot leave, that decides what you see, what you can buy, whom you can reach, and what your labor is worth, is not a tool. It is a place, and you live in it, and you did not vote on its constitution.

Watch how the dependence deepens. We outsource memory first, because it is convenient. Then navigation, because it is faster. Then judgment, because there is too much information to weigh. Then conversation, because it is easier to let a machine draft the apology. Each surrender is individually reasonable. Add them up and you have a population that can no longer do the things its ancestors did without thinking, and that cannot opt out without paying a heavy price.

This is what I mean by the tech tree turning around. For two hundred years the story was that we climb it. Now we are the fruit.

**What to do with this chapter:** Notice one thing this week that you used to do yourself and now hand to a device. Do it by hand once. Not as a purity ritual; as a measurement. How much did you lose? How much did you remember you had?

## Chapter 2 — Towers, Gigafactories, and the Hunger That Builds Them

In the desert of northwest Saudi Arabia, a line of glass is being drawn across the sand, a city planned as a single continuous skyscraper. In Texas and Brandenburg and Shanghai, factories the size of small towns turn out batteries and cars and chips. We call these projects ambitious. The older word for what they express is appetite.

I am not against building. Humans have always built, and the pyramids, the cathedrals, and the great mosques of Isfahan are among the best things we have ever done. The difference is in what the building is for. Isfahan’s domes were made to hold light and prayer. A land-scraper built to impress investors is made to hold a story about the future, which is paid for with concrete, aquifers, and the labor of people who will never live inside it.

We have become locusts. Not because locusts are wicked, but because a locust is what an organism becomes when it has no predator, no ceiling, and no memory of the last swarm. A locust is hungry in the aggregate and innocent in the particular, and the particular is where each of us lives, which is why none of us feels responsible.

The cure is not shame. Shame is a poor engine. The cure is limits we choose rather than limits that arrive as famine: a politics that can say *enough* and still be re-elected, an economics that counts a river as an asset, and a culture that finds a modest life dignified instead of defeated.

# Part II — Iran, After the Twelve Days

## Chapter 4 — June 2025

The war lasted twelve days. Twelve days is almost nothing in the life of a nation, and it is almost everything in the life of a currency.

In June 2025, Israel and Iran exchanged strikes in a conflict that drew in the United States and ended in a ceasefire that felt, to ordinary Iranians, less like peace than like a held breath. Buildings had been hit. Commanders had been killed. And a population already accustomed to wondering whether tomorrow’s prices would be survivable learned a new kind of fear, the fear that the institutions meant to protect them were visibly fallible.

Here is the part that every collapse has in common, in every country, in every century: the damage that matters most is not the damage you can photograph. It is the damage to trust. A family that believed the state could shield it learns, in one fortnight, that the state cannot even be certain of its own airspace. From that moment, every decision becomes a hedge. Convert your savings to dollars, or gold, or anything with a foreign face on it. Quietly check your cousin’s visa status. Keep the car’s tank full.

The hedging is rational for each household. Added together, it is the run on the bank that turns a bad year into a broken decade.

## Chapter 5 — The Rial Falls Through the Floor

A currency is a promise that other people will accept a piece of paper tomorrow for the same bread they would hand you today. When that promise cracks, it does not crack evenly.

Imagine the rial as a rope bridge. For years the planks have been rotting: sanctions, mismanagement, a state that spent its oil income faster than it earned it. The June war cut several ropes at once. After that, what you hear from Iranians is the same sentence in a hundred accents: *the price changed while I was in line.*

Price controls, introduced to protect the poor, produce the opposite. Fix the price of bread below what it costs to make, and the baker stops baking, or bakes less, or sells at the back door at the real price to those who can pay. The official price still appears on the shop window, a small museum exhibit of a world that no longer exists.

The poor spend most of their income on food, so they feel inflation first and longest. A professional can move money to dollars. A day laborer cannot. A pensioner on a fixed income watches it shrink in real time, and is told, on state television, that the economy is resilient.

Resilient is a lovely word. It is what we say about the person who has not yet broken.

# Part III — The Tongue

## Chapter 8 — How a Language Gets Erased

A language rarely dies from a ban. It dies from being made inconvenient.

First the schools drift toward another medium of instruction. Then the forms, the signs, the exams, the official correspondence. Then the best jobs. Then, eventually, parents decide, lovingly and sensibly, that their children will have an easier life if they speak the other tongue at home. No one ordered it. Everyone chose it. That is how a culture is quietly dismantled: by a thousand reasonable choices made under pressure.

Farsi is in no danger of vanishing from the Earth. It carries a thousand years of Rumi, Hafez, Ferdowsi, and Forugh, and tens of millions of speakers across Iran, Afghanistan, and Tajikistan, with a wide diaspora besides. But a language can be hollowed while still being spoken: stripped of its poetry, its irony, its capacity to say what is actually true. A language that may only say the approved sentence is not the language of Hafez. It is a loudspeaker with a familiar accent.

The real defense is old and humble. Write it. Sing it. Argue in it at dinner. Teach a child one poem by heart.
